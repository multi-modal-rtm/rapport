\begin{abstract}
Graph-based context modeling is the dominant architectural paradigm in multimodal emotion recognition in conversation (ERC), yet reported gains are typically measured over frozen or weakly adapted encoders. We present a controlled attribution study asking whether conversation-graph machinery --- speaker-state recurrence, relationship memory, and shift-aware objectives --- retains measurable value once the text encoder is fine-tuned. Using a residual do-no-harm design in which every component provably initializes as an identity on a frozen foundation, we find, across two benchmarks (MELD, IEMOCAP) and up to seven seeds, that on frozen features the graph stack's paired gain is $+0.0356$ (std $0.0106$), reproducing the literature premise; after fine-tuning the text encoder, even without conversational context in its input window, the gain falls to $-0.0080$ (std $0.0136$), and at the locked recipe to $-0.0088$ (std $0.0072$); and a pre-registered trial on IEMOCAP's longer dyadic dialogues, designed to favor relationship memory, instead finds a more negative isolated contribution ($-0.0334$, std $0.0284$) than on MELD ($-0.0022$, std $0.0086$), refuting rather than rescuing the mechanism. Relational memory still improves an auxiliary shift-detection task on both corpora, showing the null is not from a broken component. We release the methodology, code, and pre-registered decision history as a reusable protocol.
\end{abstract}
